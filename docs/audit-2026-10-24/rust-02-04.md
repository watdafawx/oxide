# Curriculum audit: Rust worlds 02-ownership, 03-collections, 04-errors (2026-10-24)

Scope: all 33 quests (02: 10, 03: 13, 04: 10). I read every `quest.toml` and plain `lesson.md`, diffed starter against solution for 02/01, 02/02, 02/05, 02/08, 02/09 (games), 03/06, 03/09, 04/01, 04/02, 04/06 and the boss TODO lists, and skimmed the games flavour of 02/01, 02/09, 02/10, 03/05, 04/02 and 04/07. Context I read first: `docs/CONTENT.md`, `docs/TRACKS.md`, `docs/REWRITE-rust-00-01.md`, `docs/tracks/rust-terms.md`, `PLAYTEST-2026-10-20b-rust-00-03.md`, `-20b-rust-04-06.md`, `-20-rust-03-06.md`.

**Relation to earlier reports.** The playtests asked one question: is every API call the solution needs named somewhere first? They fixed that by adding "toolbox" bullets. This audit asks something else: are the *concepts* and *syntax* explained, with the *why*, for someone who has never programmed? On that question the answer is mostly no. Part of the reason is the playtest fix itself: the toolbox bullets turned several lessons into glossaries, which `CONTENT.md` names as a bug ("so is a lesson that turns into a glossary to avoid it").

## The headline finding: a style cliff at the 01 → 02 boundary

`01-foundations` was rewritten into 28 single-idea quests in the golden shape: an intro, kicker cards, one code block, situation first and the name after, and every term glossed in bold. Worlds 02-04 were **not** rewritten. They are still in the old, dense "Rust Book in bullets" style:

- Not one plain lesson in 02-04 uses kicker cards. Most have 2-4 code blocks and 5-10 bullets.
- Almost every Your turn starts "Open `src/lib.rs`" (CONTENT.md: "No 'open the file' step"). Three end with "Press Run to play it" (02/10, 03/13, 04/10), which refers to the UI.
- Games-flavour lessons open with a `**Why now:**` label (CONTENT.md: "Intro paragraph, no label").
- Plain lessons use textbook names (Ada, Linus, garden, Point), which the plain flavour allows. But they often have no intro tying the idea to the project at all.
- `docs/tracks/rust-terms.md` has **no rows** for worlds 02-11. It jumps from `01-foundations/28` to `12-broadcast/01`. The ledger that enforces "used before taught" does not exist for these worlds.
- One-idea-per-quest is broken often. 03/09 "Text as a list" teaches about 10 things (split, trim, parse, ok, filter_map, chars, join, format width/precision, to_vec, char contains, `*c`). 04/01 teaches about 8 (error enums, implementing a trait, `fmt::Formatter`, `write!`, the `Error` trait, `is_empty`, `parse::<i64>`, `map_err`, `return Err`).

A beginner who just finished 28 gentle quests where `mut` alone was a whole quest hits 02/01, where four sentences explain ownership, moves, garbage collection and clone, and 02/04, where "lives on the heap" appears with no explanation.

**Verdict.** The lessons are accurate and the sequence (move → borrow → &mut → String/&str → slices → checker → Clone/Copy → structs → lifetimes → boss) is the standard one. The read quest 02/06 is well done. But the world never says *why* ownership exists in terms a non-programmer can follow. "Garbage collector", "heap", "frees" and "allocation" are all used without a gloss. **Worst quests**
- `02-ownership/01-moves`: the "why" is one clause ("why Rust needs no garbage collector: the compiler always knows who frees each value"), and a beginner knows neither term. It does not say why `let b = a;` worked on numbers all through world 01 (Copy is not explained until 07), so a beginner forms the wrong model "assignment breaks things". The task is one `.clone()`, and the lesson then calls clone the expensive option. `println!` and `String::from` are used for the first time, unexplained (world 01 used `format!` and `.to_string()`).
- `02-ownership/04-string-vs-str`: "It lives on the heap" is the first use of *heap* in the track, with no gloss. "Rust converts for you" (deref coercion) gives no why. Three ideas in one quest (owned vs borrowed text, coercion, `.as_str()` for match). The Your turn is "three one-liners", so it is mostly transcription.
- `02-ownership/09-lifetimes-intro`: the task is `if a.len() >= b.len() { a } else { b }`. The lifetime that is the point of the quest is pre-written. It explains `'a` as "a label (like `T` for types)", but generics `T` have not been taught (world 05). `let result;` (declare now, assign later) and the inner `{ }` block scope are both used for the first time, untaught, in the example that carries the lesson.
- `02-ownership/08-ownership-in-structs`: same problem. The learner writes `Borrowed { name: &self.name, level: self.level }`. `'_` is introduced in a Your turn bullet.
- `02-ownership/07-clone-and-copy`: "small plain structs work this way" implies structs are Copy automatically, and the next sentence says you must derive it. `#[derive(...)]` is the learner's first **attribute** and first **trait**. Neither word is explained, yet the points ask "Which trait duplicates silently…" and "Derive the traits.". The starter carries `#[derive(Debug, PartialEq)]`, never explained.
- `02-ownership/10-boss-text-adventure` (plain) and games "server console": the "Toolbox you have not met yet" introduces closures (`|n| n == "Alex"`), `iter_mut`, `position`, `remove`, `for &line in` pattern-destructuring and tuple closures before closures exist (03/06). **Best quests (models)**
- `02-ownership/06-the-borrow-checker-is-your-friend`: three questions the checker asks, six annotated lines, and the "line 6 is legal because the borrows ended" insight (non-lexical lifetimes, told plainly). It has a why, a concrete trace and a quiz decided by the text.
- `02-ownership/03-mutable-borrows`: "the caller opts in twice" is a real why. The one-writer rule is stated with its purpose. The three objectives build up.
- `02-ownership/05-slices`: the objectives teach edge cases (no space, ≤ 2 items, fewer than 3), and the empty slice and `saturating_sub` are reused well.

## World 03-collections

| Beginner clarity | Experienced-dev value | Coherence / sequencing | Task-lesson fit | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 7 | 4 | 6 | **5** |

**Verdict.** A good std-library tour for someone who already programs: `entry().or_insert`, laziness, `windows`, `filter_map` with `.ok()`, `total_cmp`, collecting into `Result<Vec<_>,_>`. For a beginner it is a wall. Tasks match lessons once you accept the glossary approach. The TODOs now say what to do, not how (improved since the playtest).

**Worst quests**

- `03-collections/05-iterator-chains`: one bullet tries to explain `&&i32` ("a reference to the `&i32` that `iter` gives"), `**w` and auto-deref on field access. To a beginner who has never been told what `*` does, this is three unknowns at once. `collect::<Vec<_>>()` shows the turbofish and `_` (infer this type) without naming either.
- `03-collections/02-vec-ops`: six methods in one block. `retain(|n| *n > 4)` uses a closure and a deref ("a later quest unpacks it"). There is no situation: "Real lists need editing" is generic.

- `03-collections/01-vec-basics`: `{:?}` (Debug printing) is used for the first time with no explanation. `Vec<T>` angle brackets are unexplained ("a Vec of T" is never said). - `03-collections/10-hashset-and-btreemap`: `into_iter()` appears with no word on how it differs from `iter()`. That difference is the most Rust-specific idea in the world (consuming vs borrowing iteration) and is never taught anywhere in 02-04. `use std::collections::{BTreeMap, HashSet}` (grouped import) is used before modules and `use` are taught (04/07).

| Beginner clarity | Experienced-dev value | Coherence / sequencing | Task-lesson fit | **Overall** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 7 | 4 | 6 | **4** |

**Verdict.** For an experienced dev this is the most valuable of the three worlds. The library/app split (thiserror vs anyhow), `From` powering `?`, root cause vs context and "if the user can cause it, return `Err`" are exactly the opinions they want, and briefly put. For a beginner it is built on concepts the track has not taught. Every quest implements or names a **trait** (`Display`, `Error`, `From`, `Into`, `Default`) with `impl X for Y` syntax, and traits are world 05. `Box` (06/01), `dyn` (05/06), generics `<A>`/`<T>` (05/03) and the heap are all used as if known. The word "trait" never appears in any 02-04 lesson, yet two points quizzes ask "Which trait…". The world is also two topics bolted together: error handling (01-06), then modules and Cargo (07-09) with a todo-list boss. The games spine (manifest loader) holds it together better than the plain flavour does.

**Worst quests**
- `04-errors/01-custom-error-enum`: the first quest of the world (CONTENT.md wants it under 5 minutes; it is 12). It has the learner write `impl fmt::Display for ParseError { fn fmt(&self, f: &mut fmt::Formatter) -> fmt::Result`. That is a trait impl, a foreign module path, a `&mut` to a library type, and a `fmt::Result` that is not the `Result` they know, all unexplained. ### Example rewrite: `02-ownership/01-moves` (plain), golden shape

> A program's text has to live somewhere in memory, and someone has to clean it up when it is no longer needed. Rust settles who that someone is before the program ever runs, and today you meet the rule.
>
> **Numbers get copied.** In the last world you wrote `let total = count;` many times and both names kept working. A `u32` is four bytes, so Rust simply copies it, and nothing needs cleaning up.
>
> **Text is different.** A `String` can be any length, so its letters live in a separate, growable area of memory called the **heap**, which means memory a program asks for while it runs and must hand back later. The `String` variable holds only a note saying where the letters are.
>
> **One owner cleans up.** If two names held the same note, both would try to hand the memory back, and the second would free memory that is already gone. So Rust gives each value exactly one **owner**, the name responsible for cleaning it up when it goes away.
>
> **Handing it over.** `let b = a;` with a `String` gives the note to `b`. This is called a **move**: `b` is now the owner, and `a` may not be used again. Passing a `String` into a function moves it the same way.
>
> ```text
> error[E0382]: borrow of moved value: `name`
> ```
>
> **Ask for a second copy.** `.clone()` makes a brand-new `String` with its own letters, so each name owns one. It costs a copy of the text; the next quest shows how to lend instead.

(The original says the same in 4 sentences. This version adds the why: what memory is, why a double free is a problem, and why numbers behaved differently. It also connects to world 01 and puts the compiler error before the fix.)

### Example card for the missing `*` quest

> **Follow the arrow.** A reference `&n` is like an arrow pointing at `n`. Most of the time Rust follows the arrow for you: `bag.items.len()` works on a `&Backpack`. But comparing or adding needs the number itself, so you write `*r`, which means "the value the arrow points at". `*r > 4` compares the number; `r > 4` compares an arrow to a number and fails to build.

## What a true non-programmer still lacks (input for a "general programming" world)

These are not taught anywhere before or inside 02-04, and the Rust lessons assume them:
- **Memory as a place**: what a variable physically is, that values have a size, stack vs heap, what "allocate" and "free" mean, and why a pointer/reference is an address. Without this, ownership is just rules.
- **What a type is for** beyond number sizes: why the compiler needs to know types, and what "generic" (a placeholder for a type) means.
- **Scope and lifetime of a variable**: a name exists from `let` to the closing `}`; blocks nest.
- **Functions as values** (passing a rule into another function) before closures.
- **Collections as concepts**: list vs lookup table vs set, and why order matters or doesn't, before `Vec`/`HashMap`/`HashSet`/`BTreeMap` APIs.
- **"Iterate", "consume", "lazy"** as general ideas, with a hand-written `for` loop translated step by step into a chain.
- **Interfaces/contracts** ("a trait is a promise a type can keep"), needed in world 04 before world 05.
- **Errors as values vs crashes** in general terms (02/03 of world 04 is a good start).
- **Text encoding**: bytes vs characters, why `"é"` is two bytes.
- **Files, paths, the working directory**, and what reading or writing a file can fail on.
- **Libraries/packages and versions** (what a dependency is) before using `thiserror`/`anyhow`.
- **Reading compiler errors as a skill** (00/02 starts this; 02 relies on it heavily: E0382, E0499, E0502, E0106 deserve a tour).
- **Debug printing** (`{:?}`, `dbg!`) as a tool for seeing what a program is doing.
